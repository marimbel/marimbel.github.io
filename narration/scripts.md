## index.html  →  save as `audio/index.mp3`

Welcome to my research tutorial on temporal filtering and keypoint trajectory stabilization for human pose estimation in exercise videos. Modern pose estimators look at each video frame almost independently, so the skeleton they output shakes, drops joints, and sometimes swaps them. That noise becomes a big problem when an exercise app tries to measure a knee angle, count repetitions, or warn you about bad form. In this tutorial we will look at why the jitter happens, then work through the filters people use to fix it: moving averages, exponential smoothing, the One Euro filter, and the Kalman filter. We will also cover how to handle missing keypoints, the tradeoff between smoothness and lag, and an experiment comparing all of the filters on a squat sequence. Along the way there is an interactive filter lab, a live webcam lab, and a short quiz. The whole tutorial takes about twenty to thirty minutes.

## 01-intro.html  →  save as `audio/01-intro.mp3`

Section one. Before we can fix jitter, we need to understand where it comes from. Most pose estimators are trained on still images, so even when they run on video they mostly estimate each frame on its own. Small changes between frames, like motion blur, a sleeve covering the elbow, or the person turning sideways, move the predicted keypoint a few pixels back and forth even when the person is standing still. A few pixels does not sound like much, but exercise apps compute angles from three keypoints and speeds from differences between frames. Both of those operations amplify noise. A two degree wobble in a knee angle becomes almost seventy degrees per second of fake angular velocity at thirty frames per second. That is why apps that skip temporal filtering count extra repetitions and flash false form warnings. The rest of this tutorial is about building the filter layer that prevents this.

## 02-trajectories.html  →  save as `audio/02-trajectories.mp3`

Section two. To filter keypoints we first have to treat them as signals. A single joint over time is a trajectory: a list of x and y positions, sometimes a depth value z, and a confidence score for every frame. It helps to normalize these coordinates, for example by torso length, so a filter tuned for one person or camera distance works for another. From positions we can compute velocity and acceleration with finite differences. These are exactly what the One Euro and Kalman filters use, but they are also very sensitive to noise. Finally, we will write down a simple noise model with four parts: fast white jitter, slow drift, single-frame spikes, and gaps. Every filter in this tutorial can be judged by which of those four parts it handles.

## 03-simple-smoothing.html  →  save as `audio/03-simple-smoothing.mp3`

Section three covers the simplest filters, which are also the baseline every fancier method has to beat. A moving average replaces each sample with the mean of the last N samples. It is very effective at removing jitter, but it always runs N minus one over two frames behind the real motion. The exponential moving average keeps a single running value and blends each new sample in with a weight alpha. It uses almost no memory, and its lag is about one minus alpha over alpha frames. Gaussian and Savitzky-Golay filters look at future frames as well as past ones. That makes them zero-lag, which is great for analyzing a recorded video, but impossible for live feedback. The key lesson is that all of these use a fixed amount of smoothing, so they are either too jittery when you are still or too slow when you move fast.

## 04-one-euro.html  →  save as `audio/04-one-euro.mp3`

Section four is about the One Euro filter, published by Casiez, Roussel and Vogel at CHI in 2012. The idea is simple. It is an exponential moving average whose cutoff frequency is not fixed. When the keypoint moves slowly, the cutoff is low, so jitter is removed. When the keypoint moves fast, the cutoff rises, so the filter follows the motion with little lag. To do that, the filter estimates the speed of the signal, smooths that speed with its own small low-pass filter, and then sets the cutoff to a minimum value plus beta times the speed. There are only two parameters you really tune: min cutoff, which controls jitter at rest, and beta, which controls lag during fast movement. We will walk through the tuning procedure the authors recommend, and one trap: beta depends on the units of your coordinates.

## 05-kalman.html  →  save as `audio/05-kalman.mp3`

Section five introduces the Kalman filter, which takes a different approach from the One Euro filter. Instead of only smoothing, the Kalman filter keeps a small model of how the joint moves. In our case the state is the joint's position and velocity, and we assume the velocity stays roughly constant from one frame to the next. Every frame has two steps. First, predict: move the state forward using the velocity, and grow the uncertainty. Second, update: compare the prediction with the new measurement, and blend them using the Kalman gain, which depends on how uncertain each one is. Because it has a prediction step, the Kalman filter can keep going when a keypoint is missing, it can down-weight low-confidence measurements, and it can reject outliers that are too far from the prediction. The cost is more math, two noise parameters to tune, and overshoot when a movement stops suddenly.

## 06-missing-keypoints.html  →  save as `audio/06-missing-keypoints.mp3`

Section six is about the frames where the detector does not give us a usable keypoint at all. The first decision is a confidence threshold: below it, we treat the keypoint as missing rather than feeding garbage into the filter. Then we have three choices. We can hold the last good value, which is simple but freezes the joint. We can interpolate between the good values on both sides of the gap, which is accurate but only works after the fact. Or we can predict through the gap with a motion model like the Kalman filter, which works live but drifts if the gap is long. Finally, we need a rule for when to stop guessing. If a gap is too long, or the estimate violates the body's geometry, the honest answer is to mark the joint as lost and tell the app, instead of inventing motion.

## 07-smoothness-latency.html  →  save as `audio/07-smoothness-latency.mp3`

Section seven is about the central tradeoff of this whole topic: smoothness costs time. Every causal filter averages the present with the past, so its output is always a little behind. For a moving average of seven frames that delay is a hundred milliseconds. Lag is not only a display problem. When the filter is late during a fast squat, it also cuts off the bottom of the movement. In our experiment the moving average and the exponential filter reported squats five to seven degrees shallower than they really were. So the right amount of smoothing depends on the exercise: slow, controlled movements can afford heavy smoothing, and explosive movements cannot. We will finish with the real-time budget on a phone, where the filter itself is almost free, but the latency it adds is not.

## 08-exercise-analysis.html  →  save as `audio/08-exercise-analysis.mp3`

Section eight connects the filters to what an exercise app actually does with them. First, joint angles. Once the hip, knee and ankle are stable, the knee angle is stable too, and it becomes the main signal for squats and lunges. Second, rep counting. A good counter uses two thresholds instead of one, which is called hysteresis, so that jitter near a single line cannot be counted as an extra rep. With the raw signal, a single-threshold counter found twelve reps when there were only ten. Third, phase detection: splitting each rep into descent, bottom, ascent and top using the sign of angular velocity, which only works if velocity is smooth. And fourth, form feedback. Requiring an error to persist for several frames before warning the user removes most false alerts. We finish with the complete pipeline in code.

## 09-experiment.html  →  save as `audio/09-experiment.mp3`

Section nine puts all of the filters side by side on the same squat sequence. To measure error we need to know the true knee angle, and a real video does not come with that. So I built a twenty second side-view squat with known ground truth: four slow squats, four seconds standing still, and six fast squats. Then I added the kinds of errors real detectors make: frame jitter, slow wobble, spikes, a motion blur burst, and a short dropout. I ran seven filters on it and measured jitter while standing, overall angle error, lag on the fast reps, worst-case error, rep counts, and run time. The short version: no causal filter wins every column. The One Euro filter is the best balance of smoothness and lag, the Kalman filter has the lowest error and handles the dropout, and the offline filters show how much better you can do if you can wait. The same scripts can be run on your own MediaPipe video, and the interactive lab lets you change the parameters yourself.

## 10-limitations.html  →  save as `audio/10-limitations.mp3`

Our final section is about what temporal filtering cannot do, and where research is going. Filters smooth what the detector gives them. If a joint is hidden for a whole second, or the detector confidently swaps the left and right knee, a filter alone cannot recover the truth. A single camera also has a depth problem. A two-dimensional knee angle is the projection of a three-dimensional angle, and it changes with camera position even when the movement does not. Research is moving from hand-designed filters to learned temporal models. SmoothNet is a small network that learns to remove jitter from any pose estimator's output. And a CVPR 2026 paper, HTD-Refine, predicts velocities and accelerations directly from video to avoid both jitter and over-smoothing. I will close with the labs doing this work and what I would try next.

## lab.html  →  save as `audio/lab.mp3`

Welcome to the filter lab. This page runs the same simulated squat sequence from section nine, and the same four live filters, right in your browser. Pick a filter from the menu and move its sliders. The chart updates immediately, and the numbers underneath show jitter while standing, overall angle error, lag on the fast reps, and the rep count. Use the zoom buttons to look at one part of the clip: the slow squats, standing still, the fast squats, the motion blur, or the dropout. Press play to watch the stick figures. The left figure is the raw detector output and the right one is filtered. Try to find settings that keep the right figure still while standing, without falling behind on the fast reps. That balance is the whole point of this tutorial.

## webcam.html  →  save as `audio/webcam.mp3`

This is the live webcam lab. It runs Google's MediaPipe pose landmarker directly in your browser, on your own camera or on a video file you choose. Nothing is uploaded; all processing happens on your computer. Stand sideways to the camera so your whole body is visible, choose which leg is closer to the camera, and press start. The gray skeleton is the raw output of the detector, and the blue skeleton is filtered. The chart on the right shows your knee angle over the last six seconds, raw and filtered, and the counter below counts squats using the filtered angle. Try standing still and watch how much the raw knee angle shakes, then do a few fast squats and change the filter settings to see the tradeoff between smoothness and lag on real data.
